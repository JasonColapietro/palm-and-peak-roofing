Hurricane-season content calendar for a roofing website: what to publish before June 1

A month-by-month plan for the storm content a Palm Beach County roofer should have live, linked and tested before the season opens.

By Jason Colapietro, Suede AI · November 15, 2026 · West Palm Beach, Florida

A roofer's storm content gets judged in the hours after a hurricane warning, but it gets decided in February. Anything that is not written, linked and tested by June 1 will be drafted in a hurry, on a phone, while the crews are out tarping roofs.

This is the pre-season calendar Suede AI uses when planning content for a contractor website, shown against Palm and Peak Roofing, the fictional concept Jason Colapietro designed in West Palm Beach. Our earlier note on storm season web pages covers how inspection and storm pages should be structured. This one covers when each piece ships, and what has to be true about it before the season opens.

Work backwards from June 1, then move the deadline up

The Atlantic hurricane season runs from June 1 through November 30. A page you change on May 31 may not be recrawled and reevaluated for days or weeks, and AI answer engines that lean on search indexes inherit that delay. So the working deadline for a Palm Beach County roofer is May 15, with the last two weeks of May held back as buffer for corrections.

Jason Colapietro puts it to Suede clients as a single rule: after Memorial Day, only fixes ship. New pages, redesigns and fresh sections all belong to the months before.

January and February: collect the questions you already answered

The best source for storm content is not a keyword tool. It is last season's phone notes, contact form submissions and emails. Export them, strip the personal details, and group every message by the question underneath it.

You will end up with a short list that repeats. In West Palm Beach and Jupiter it tends to look like this: is a lifted shingle an emergency, should I wait for the adjuster before calling a roofer, do you reach my neighborhood, and how soon can someone look. Rank the questions by how often they came in, then write next to each one which page on your site should answer it. If no page can, that gap is your first content task of the year.

February is also audit month. For every page that matters in a storm, write down when its text last changed in a meaningful way, check that the phone number matches your Google Business Profile exactly, and click every internal link.

March: rewrite the evergreen pages, not the news

With the question list in hand, rewrite the inspection and storm pages so the most frequent questions become headings and each answer can stand on its own. Suede Signal's guide on how to write passages AI answer engines can quote explains why a two or three sentence answer under a plain question heading is the unit that gets lifted into AI overviews. Write it so it would still be correct if a homeowner in Palm Beach Gardens read only that paragraph.

Then decide whether the list justifies one new evergreen page, and only one. A page that answers "what should I document before a roofer arrives" earns its place if dozens of people asked a version of it last year. A page about a topic nobody asked about does not. Update the dateModified in your structured data and the lastmod in your sitemap only when the words actually changed. Bumping dates on untouched pages teaches crawlers to ignore them.

April: write storm-week drafts and leave them unpublished

The most useful work in April produces nothing visible. Prepare four drafts in your CMS, or as files in the repository for a static site like this concept, and leave them unpublished:

  1. A dated service-status note. A fill-in template: "Updated [date and time]: our crews are currently reaching [areas]. Phone lines are open [hours]." Only facts go in the blanks.
  2. A site-wide banner that links to the storm page. A banner, not a popup, so it does not block the content a worried reader came for.
  3. A short social post that points back to the storm page, so post-storm traffic lands on a page you control and can correct.
  4. Approved wording for the limits. One paragraph that tells homeowners to check coverage questions with their own insurance carrier, written calmly in advance so nobody improvises an insurance promise under pressure.

Then decide who is allowed to publish, and test that the person can actually log in from a phone on a cellular connection. Store the login in a shared password manager, not on one office laptop that might be without power.

May: test as if the power is out

May is for proving the plan works. A simple test run:

  • Load the storm page with network throttling set to a slow mobile profile in your browser's developer tools. The first readable sentence should appear within a few seconds. The Palm and Peak pages are plain HTML and CSS with one compressed image, which is why they pass this test easily.
  • Confirm the phone number is a tappable tel: link and is written out in text, not inside an image.
  • Hand a phone to someone outside the company and ask them to reach the storm page from the home page. Two taps is the target.
  • Ask two or three AI assistants what a homeowner in West Palm Beach should do after hurricane roof damage, and note which sources they cite. The point is not to game the answer. It shows you whether your page is part of the conversation at all.
  • Resubmit the sitemap in Google Search Console once the March and April changes are live.

The same discipline applies to any business whose pages change with the weather. The Blueline Surf concept's plan for surf report, rentals and lessons pages shows a Jupiter shop keeping one page per question, so a change in conditions updates one page instead of ten. A roofer's storm page works the same way.

What to leave out before the season

  • Fear content. "Is your roof ready for the big one?" with no specifics helps nobody and reads as an ad.
  • Unsourced numbers. If you mention the seasonal outlook, link the NOAA outlook itself, which is usually issued in late May, and put a date next to it.
  • Insurance advice beyond pointing people to their own policy and carrier.
  • Placeholder town pages. A page for every town in the county with the same three paragraphs is a doorway pattern, and AI systems have no reason to cite it.
