Storm season web pages: how Florida roofers should structure inspection and storm-service content

A practical layout for inspection and storm restoration pages, drawn from the Palm and Peak Roofing concept that Suede AI built in West Palm Beach.

By Jason Colapietro, Suede AI · November 14, 2026 · West Palm Beach, Florida

Atlantic hurricane season opens every June 1 and runs through November 30, and for roofers in Palm Beach County the busiest web traffic of the year arrives in the hours after a storm passes. Homeowners search from phones on weak connections, asking whether a stain or a missing tile means trouble. The pages that help them in that moment have to be written months before the storm.

This note uses the inspection and storm restoration pages from the Palm and Peak Roofing concept as a worked example. Palm and Peak is fictional, a portfolio piece by Suede AI, so treat the structure as the lesson, not the business.

Keep inspection and storm pages separate

An inspection page and a storm page serve different moments. Inspection searches are planned: a home purchase in Jupiter, an aging roof in West Palm Beach, a pre-season check. Storm searches are urgent and specific: something changed last night and the homeowner wants to know what to do first.

Folding both into one "roof services" page muddies each intent. Search engines have a harder time matching the page to either query, and AI answer engines, which usually quote one passage, may pull the wrong one. The concept gives each intent its own page with its own focus list, process and questions, then links the two together. When Suede reviews a contractor site, a merged inspection-and-storm page is one of the first things we flag.

A structure that works for people and answer engines

Both concept pages use the same five blocks. We recommend it for any Florida roofer:

  1. A one-sentence lede. "A clear path from post-storm questions to a considered repair plan for your home." It tells the reader and the crawler exactly what the page is about.
  2. What gets reviewed. Visible roof damage, leaks and affected areas, and repair scope. Three items, each with one sentence.
  3. A numbered process. Assess the property, document the observations, agree on next steps. Numbers reduce anxiety because they show the homeowner there is an order to things.
  4. Real questions in the homeowner's words. "Does storm damage always mean replacing the roof?" is a better heading than "Our Storm Philosophy".
  5. Related links. Send storm readers to inspections and back, plus a route home.

Write each FAQ answer so it makes sense on its own, in two or three sentences, without "as mentioned above". That is the passage an AI overview is most likely to quote, and it should be correct when quoted out of context.

Safety copy is content, not legal filler

The most useful line on the concept's storm page is short: share photos taken from a safe location and avoid climbing onto a roof. Homeowners genuinely wonder whether they should go up and look, and a clear answer prevents injuries and gives the page something specific to be found for.

The same page lists what helps after a storm: the approximate date, what the homeowner noticed, photos from the ground and records of prior roof work. That checklist is useful whether or not the reader ever hires you, which is exactly why it ranks and gets cited. What the page does not do is promise insurance outcomes, quote claim timelines or imply approval by any insurer. Those questions belong with the homeowner's policy and carrier, and saying so builds more trust than guessing.

A publishing calendar for hurricane season

  • April and May: review the inspection page, refresh the FAQ from questions your office actually received last year, and test both pages on a throttled mobile connection.
  • June through November: after a storm, add a short dated update only if it is true, for example which parts of Palm Beach County your crews are currently reaching. Remove it when it stops being current.
  • December: write down what people asked during the season and fold the best questions into the evergreen pages.

Avoid spinning up a thin page for every named storm. A dozen near-identical "Hurricane X roof repair" pages look like doorway pages to Google and add nothing an AI system would want to cite. One strong storm page that you keep updated beats twelve stale ones. Jason Colapietro's rule of thumb for Suede clients is simple: if you would not send the page to a neighbor after a storm, do not publish it.

Build for bad network conditions

After a storm, cell networks are congested and some homes are on backup power. The concept ships one compressed WebP hero image and otherwise plain HTML and CSS, so the storm page stays readable on a weak signal. Jason Colapietro brings this habit from mobile work: he has shipped nine iOS apps under his own Apple developer account, and you learn quickly to assume the network will fail. If your storm page loads a video background, a chat widget and three tracking scripts, it is working against the people who need it most.
